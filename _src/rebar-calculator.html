How to use the rebar calculator

  1. Enter the slab's length and width — the outside dimensions of the forms.
  2. Choose the bar size and spacing from your plans. If there are no plans, #3 or #4 bars at 12 to 18 inches on center are typical for driveways, patios and garage slabs on good ground.
  3. Set the distance from the edges so the bars stay inside the concrete, and the stock length your supplier sells.
  4. Check the lap splice. Bars longer than one stock length are spliced by overlapping two bars; the calculator adds that overlap and works out how to cut your sticks.

How the rebar grid is calculated

Bar run lengths = slab length − 2 × edge distance (and the same for the width) Bars along length = (width − 2 × edge) ÷ spacing, rounded down, + 1 Bars along width = (length − 2 × edge) ÷ spacing, rounded down, + 1 Lap splice = lap (bar diameters) × bar diameter Weight = total length × weight per foot

Worked example: a 24 × 20 ft garage slab with #4 bars at 18 in, 3 in from the edges

  1. Bars running the 24 ft way: (234 in ÷ 18) + 1 = 14 bars of 23.5 ft
  2. Bars running the 20 ft way: (282 in ÷ 18 = 15.7 → 15) + 1 = 16 bars of 19.5 ft
  3. Each 23.5 ft run is longer than a 20 ft stick, so it needs one splice: 40 × ½ in = 20 in of overlap, making 25.2 ft of bar per run
  4. Total: 14 × 25.2 + 16 × 19.5 = 664 ft of #4 bar, about 444 lb
  5. Sticks: one per 19.5 ft bar (16), one full stick plus a 5.2 ft piece per 23.5 ft run (14 + 5 sticks cut into three pieces each) = 35 sticks of 20 ft, with 224 intersections to tie

Placing rebar in a slab

  • Keep it off the ground. Rebar lying on the dirt does nothing. Support the grid on chairs or concrete blocks ("dobies") so it sits in the middle or upper part of the slab, and don't rely on pulling it up during the pour.
  • Keep it covered. Bars too close to the surface or the edges can rust and crack the concrete. Concrete cast against the ground needs about 3 inches of cover to the earth.
  • Tie the intersections with tie wire so the grid doesn't shift while concrete is placed — every intersection, or every other one on large slabs.
  • Overlap splices by the lap length and stagger them so they don't all line up in one place.

Rebar or wire mesh? Both help hold cracks tight; neither stops a slab from cracking. Rebar is stiffer and easier to keep at the right height, which is why many contractors prefer it for driveways. Control joints, a well-compacted base and the right slab thickness matter just as much.

Frequently asked questions

How much rebar do I need for a 10 × 10 slab?

With #4 bars at 12 inches and 3 inches from the edges, you need 10 bars of 9.5 ft each way — 20 bars and 190 ft of rebar, about 127 lb. That's 20 sticks of 10 ft, or 10 of 20 ft cut in half.

What size rebar for a driveway?

#3 or #4 bars at 12 to 18 inches on center are common for residential driveways on well-prepared ground. Heavier loads, poor soil or local code may call for more — follow your plans or ask a local contractor.

How long should rebar overlap?

A common rule of thumb is 40 bar diameters — 20 inches for #4 bar — but the required lap depends on the bar size, concrete strength and design. Structural drawings take precedence.

How much does rebar weigh?

#3 weighs 0.376 lb per foot, #4 weighs 0.668 lb and #5 weighs 1.043 lb. A 20 ft stick of #4 is about 13.4 lb.
