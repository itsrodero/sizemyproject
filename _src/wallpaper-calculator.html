How to use the wallpaper calculator

  1. Choose a whole room or one wall and enter its size and the wall height.
  2. Skip full-height openings like doors and floor-to-ceiling windows by entering their total width. Leave smaller windows in: the offcuts above and below them are rarely usable.
  3. Select the roll size printed on the label and the pattern repeat — the vertical distance before the design repeats.

How wallpaper is calculated: the strip method

Dividing the wall area by the roll area underestimates, because the end of every roll is too short for a full strip. Installers count strips instead:

Strips = wall width ÷ roll width (round up) Strip length = wall height + pattern repeat + 4 in for trimming Strips per roll = roll length ÷ strip length (round down) Rolls = strips ÷ strips per roll (round up)

Worked example: a 12 × 10 ft room, 8 ft walls, one door, 20.5 in × 33 ft rolls

  1. Wall width: 44 ft − 3 ft door = 41 ft = 492 in → 492 ÷ 20.5 = 24 strips
  2. Strip length with no repeat: 96 + 0 + 4 = 100 in; a 396 in roll gives 3 strips
  3. Rolls: 24 ÷ 3 = 8 rolls
  4. With a 21 in pattern repeat, each strip is 121 in: still 3 per roll → 8 rolls. With a 36 in repeat, strips are 136 in and only 2 fit per roll → 12 rolls. A large repeat can add several rolls.

Pattern repeat and match

  • Random match: any strip lines up with any other — least waste. Enter 0.
  • Straight match: the pattern lines up horizontally across strips; you lose up to one repeat per strip.
  • Drop match: the pattern shifts half a repeat on alternate strips; plan for the full repeat per strip.

Frequently asked questions

How many rolls of wallpaper for a 12 × 12 room?

With 8 ft walls, one door and 20.5 in × 33 ft rolls with no repeat: 45 ft of wall width → 27 strips at 3 per roll → 9 double rolls.

Should I subtract windows?

Only large or full-height ones. Pieces above and below average windows are too short to use elsewhere, so they don't save rolls.

Should I buy an extra roll?

For patterned papers, yes — a spare from the same batch is valuable for mistakes and future repairs, and a later batch may not match.
