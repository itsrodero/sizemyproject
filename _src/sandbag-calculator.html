How to use the sandbag calculator

  1. Measure the length of the barrier along the ground — around a building, across a driveway, or the width of a doorway.
  2. Choose the height: the expected water level plus about a foot of extra height, as emergency management offices commonly recommend.
  3. Pick the type. Anything taller than a foot should be a levee (pyramid) with a base about three times as wide as it is high. A single row works only for low barriers such as a doorway.

How many sandbags per 100 feet

The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers publishes these planning figures for a sandbag levee built as a pyramid with a base three times its height:

The calculator scales these figures to your length and to heights in between: they follow bags per foot = 4.5 × height² + 1.5 × height, with the height in feet. Sand assumes about 60 filled bags per cubic yard.

Worked example: a barrier 50 ft long and 2 ft high

  1. Bags per foot: 4.5 × 2² + 1.5 × 2 = 21
  2. 50 ft × 21 = 1,050 sandbags
  3. Sand: 1,050 ÷ 60 ≈ 17.5 cubic yards — about 24 tons of dry sand

How much sand to buy

A filled sandbag holds about 35 to 40 lb of sand, so roughly 60 bags use one cubic yard (about 1.35 tons of dry sand). For a few dozen sandbags, bagged sand from a home center is often easier than a bulk delivery:

  • 50 lb bags of sand: one bag fills one to one and a third sandbags, so plan on 75 to 90 bags of sand per 100 sandbags. The calculator uses the higher figure so you don't run short.
  • Bulk sand: for more than a couple of hundred sandbags, a delivery of loose sand by the cubic yard or ton costs far less per sandbag. See the sand calculator and cubic yards to tons.
  • Free sand: many cities and counties open self-fill sandbag stations before forecast floods. Check your local emergency management office.

Filling and placing sandbags

  • Fill bags one-half to two-thirds full — about 35 to 40 lb. Overfilled bags don't bed against each other and leave gaps that let water through.
  • Use the right size bag. The most common sandbags are about 14 × 26 inches.
  • Lay bags lengthwise along the flow, fold the open end under, and overlap each bag about half over the one beneath. Tamp each bag into place.
  • Stagger the joints between courses, like bricks.
  • For doorways, lay plastic sheeting under and on the water side of a low wall of bags, and keep it short — sandbags slow water down but don't make a watertight seal.

Plan ahead: sandbag barriers take far longer to build than most people expect. Many towns hand out sand and empty bags before a forecast flood — check with your local emergency management office, and follow evacuation orders over any barrier.

Frequently asked questions

How many sandbags do I need for a doorway?

For a 3 ft doorway and a 1 ft high single-row wall, about 9 bags (3 bags per foot), with plastic sheeting under and in front of them.

How many sandbags are in a ton of sand?

Roughly 45 bags filled half to two-thirds full, at about 1.35 tons per cubic yard of dry sand and 60 bags per cubic yard.

How much does a filled sandbag weigh?

About 35 to 40 lb when filled half to two-thirds full with sand — heavier if the sand is wet.

How many sandbags for a garage door?

For a 16 ft garage door, a 1 ft single-row wall takes about 48 bags, and a 1 ft levee about 96. Lay plastic sheeting under and on the water side of the bags.

How many 50 lb bags of sand do I need to fill sandbags?

Plan on 75 to 90 bags of sand per 100 sandbags: one 50 lb bag fills one to one and a third sandbags, depending on how full you fill them. The calculator uses the higher figure so you don't run short.

Can I use dirt instead of sand?

Sand is easiest to fill and shape, but many emergency guides accept local soil when sand isn't available. Avoid very loose gravel or organic soil, which let water through.

How high can a sandbag wall be?

Single rows are for low barriers. Taller barriers need a levee with a base about three times the height; for anything over a few feet, follow the guidance of your local emergency managers.
