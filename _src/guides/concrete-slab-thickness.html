Typical thickness by project

Walkways and sidewalks4 inFoot traffic only
Patios4 inThicker under heavy features like outdoor kitchens
Shed floors4 inThicken the edges for the walls
Residential driveways4–5 in5 in for trucks, soft soil or occasional heavy loads
RV, boat or dumpster pads5–6 inPlus a well-compacted base
Hot tub pads4–6 inCheck the spa manufacturer's requirement — a filled tub is very heavy
Interior floor slabs3½ in minimumThe International Residential Code's minimum for slab-on-ground floors

These are common practice for typical soils in residential work. Plans, local codes and a structural engineer's design always take priority, especially for foundations and anything carrying a building.

The payoff is large, though: the Tennessee Concrete Association notes that going from 4 to 5 inches on a driveway adds roughly 20% to the concrete cost but nearly 50% to its load-carrying capacity.

The base matters as much as the slab

Concrete cracks when the ground beneath it moves or settles unevenly. A thick slab on soft, wet or poorly compacted soil can fail sooner than a thinner one on a good base.

  • Remove topsoil and organic material — it compresses over time.
  • Compact the subgrade and add a layer of compacted gravel, commonly about 4 inches, to give a uniform, well-drained support.
  • Deal with water: slope the slab away from buildings and make sure water doesn't collect under or around it.

Reinforcement

Steel reinforcement doesn't stop concrete from cracking — it holds the pieces tightly together when it does. Common options for residential flatwork are welded wire mesh, rebar (often #3 or #4 bars in a grid) or synthetic fibers mixed into the concrete. Whichever you use, the steel must sit in the middle of the slab on chairs or supports; mesh left lying at the bottom does little.

Control joints

Concrete shrinks as it cures and will crack somewhere. Control joints tell it where. A widely used rule of thumb is to space joints no more than 2 to 3 times the slab thickness in feet — for a 4-inch slab, every 8 to 12 feet — and to cut them about a quarter of the slab's depth. Keep the panels roughly square; long, narrow panels crack across the middle.

Thickened edges

Edges carry the most stress from wheels and from the walls of a shed or garage. Many slabs are thickened by an extra 2 inches or so along the edges, tapering back into the slab over 8 to 12 inches. Add that extra volume when you calculate the order — model it as a separate footing in the concrete calculator.

Frequently asked questions

Is 3 inches of concrete enough for a patio?

It's thinner than the usual 4 inches and more likely to crack, especially on a less-than-perfect base. Four inches is the common standard for patios and walkways.

How thick should a concrete driveway be?

At least 4 inches for cars; 5 inches if you park trucks or RVs or the soil is soft. Thickened edges help too.

Does a thicker slab need a thicker base?

Not necessarily — the base's job is uniform support and drainage. Poor or wet soils call for a thicker, well-compacted base regardless of slab thickness.
